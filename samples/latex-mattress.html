The humid bedroom

You spend a third of your life on your mattress. In a humid Australian bedroom, so does a colony of dust mites.

They feed on the skin you shed, they thrive in the warm, damp pocket your body creates overnight, and their droppings are one of the most common allergy and asthma triggers in coastal Australia. The mattress you sleep on decides how comfortable you make them.

You probably know the morning pattern already.

You wake with a blocked nose that clears by lunchtime, your eyes itch for the first hour, and you sneeze more in bed than anywhere else in the house.

Plenty of people put it down to hay fever season, a dusty house or simply getting older.

The Australasian Society of Clinical Immunology and Allergy points to a more specific culprit.

ASCIA is the peak professional body for allergy specialists in Australia and New Zealand.

House dust mites are the most common allergen source in humid areas such as coastal cities and towns, and levels tend to be higher in homes kept above 20°C with poor ventilation.

That description fits a typical bedroom in Sydney, Brisbane or the Gold Coast for most of the year.

And the mattress is only where the story starts.

When researchers at the University of Manchester cultured ten used pillows, synthetic pillows grew more species of fungi than feather ones, and more of the mould Aspergillus fumigatus.

We make our pillows from natural latex for the same reasons we make our mattress from it.

How it happens

Why a warm, damp mattress suits dust mites so well

Start with a strange little fact.

Dust mites do not drink.

They pull the moisture they need straight out of the air around them, which is why ASCIA links them so closely to humidity.

Now think about what your body does for eight hours every night.

It gives off heat and moisture, and all of it has to go somewhere.

A mattress that lets that warm, damp air escape dries out during the day.

A mattress that holds on to it keeps a small tropical climate going a few centimetres below your face.

Memory foam was designed to soften with body heat and mould itself around you.

People love that feeling, and we understand why.

The trouble is that the same density that gives you the hug also makes the foam slow to let heat go.

So many foam brands now add gel beads and cooling layers to make up for it.

Heat, though, is only half of what we found when we went through the research.

The other half surprised us.

When engineers at the University of Texas tested twenty crib mattresses, every single one released volatile organic compounds.

Warmed to body temperature, the mattresses gave off about two and a half times more than they did at room temperature.

Polyurethane foam released the widest range of compounds.

The air in the sleeper's breathing zone also carried higher concentrations than the rest of the room, which makes sense once you picture where your nose sits at night.

No mattress is completely free of emissions, and we will not pretend ours is.

What you can choose is what the core is made of, how well it lets heat out, and whether you can wash away what builds up on the surface.

So what does a bed look like when it is built around those three choices?

"What about latex allergy?"

Take it seriously.

Latex allergy is caused by proteins in natural rubber.

Our latex is washed repeatedly during manufacture, which removes most of those proteins, and the core is sealed inside the cotton cover and the barrier protector, so your skin never touches it.

If you have a diagnosed latex allergy, please speak to your doctor or allergist before buying any latex product, ours included.

A note for parents

If you are choosing a bed for a child

Researchers who followed a group of children from birth, in a study published in the New England Journal of Medicine, found that children with a family history of allergy who were exposed to high levels of dust mite allergen at age one or two were about five times more likely to have asthma by age eleven.

For babies, safety comes first.

Red Nose Australia advises that babies sleep on a firm, flat mattress that fits the cot properly, with no pillows, doonas, bumpers or soft toys.

Bellbird does not make cot mattresses.

We make single and king single mattresses for children who have moved into a big bed, with the same washable cover and barrier protector as our adult range.

Before you order

Questions people ask us

Will it work on my current base?

Any solid or slatted base works, as long as the slats are no more than 7 cm apart.

Latex is heavy enough that widely spaced slats will let it dip between them.

Does latex stop dust mites completely?

No mattress can promise that, and anyone who does is overselling.

What Bellbird does is make the bed cooler, drier and washable, and wrap it in a barrier protector, which together follow the allergen advice ASCIA gives Australians.

If you have asthma or severe allergies, keep following your doctor's plan.

How often should I wash the cover?

ASCIA advises washing sheets and pillowcases weekly above 60°C and washing mattress and pillow covers at least every two months.

Our cover is built to handle both.

Is the latex natural or synthetic?

The latex layers are natural rubber, and the core comes from a single supplier, so every batch is traceable.

We do not blend in synthetic latex to cut costs.
