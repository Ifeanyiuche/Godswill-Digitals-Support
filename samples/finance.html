The Payoff Order
For people carrying more than one balance

Minimum payments are designed to be affordable. That is exactly what makes them so expensive.

The number on your statement was calculated to be comfortable enough that you keep paying it for a very long time. Nobody is hiding this. It is just never explained to you.

You are not bad with money.

You pay what is due, every month, on time. You have not missed anything. You do the sensible thing and put a little extra toward whichever balance is bothering you most that week.

And the total has barely moved in two years.

That is not a discipline problem. It is an arithmetic problem, and it comes down to something most people were never taught. The order you pay your debts in changes what they cost you, sometimes by more than the amount of a decent used car.

Same income. Same payments. Different order, and years come off the end.

Why the total refuses to move

Interest is charged on the balance you carry, so the first portion of every payment goes to the lender before a penny touches what you owe. On a card charging twenty two percent, a large share of a minimum payment is rent on money you already spent.

Spread that across four balances, and something worse happens. You pay a little extra into all of them, which feels responsible and productive, and it means none of them ever clears. Nothing gets closed, so no payment ever gets freed up to attack the next one. You stay busy and you stay still.

What you get

This is not a course about budgeting, and there is no section telling you to give up coffee. It is a system for working out your own payoff order, then holding to it.

The Payoff Calculator

Spreadsheet, works in Excel or Google Sheets

Enter your balances, rates and minimums. It returns the exact order to clear them in, your debt free date, and what the order saves you against what you are doing now.

Why it matters: most people are shocked by the date. Seeing it move when you change one input is what makes the plan stick.

The Snowball Schedule

Printable, one page

A month by month plan showing exactly what to pay where. When one balance clears, its payment rolls into the next one automatically, so the plan accelerates on its own.

Why it matters: it removes the monthly decision. You are not choosing where the extra goes ever again.

The Rate Reduction Scripts

Word for word, four scripts

What to say when you phone a card issuer to ask for a lower rate, including what to do when the first person says no. Roughly a third of people who ask get something.

Why it matters: one successful call can take a year off the plan, and it costs you fifteen minutes.

The Order of Operations Guide

Short read, forty pages

Where an emergency fund fits, when to pause retirement contributions and when not to, how to handle a car loan against a card, and which debts to leave alone entirely.

Why it matters: the common mistakes here cost more than the interest does. Clearing a low rate car loan before a high rate card is the usual one.

What waiting another year costs

Interest does not pause while you think about it. On $24,000 at the rates most cards charge, another twelve months of minimums adds somewhere near $2,800 to the total, and pushes the finish line further out than twelve months, because the balance you are still carrying keeps earning against you.

The plan gets more expensive the longer it goes unmade. That is the whole reason this is worth doing on a Tuesday evening rather than next year.

Fair questions

Is this a consolidation loan or a credit repair service?

Neither. Nobody looks at your credit, nothing is refinanced, and no third party is involved. It is a method and a set of tools you use yourself.

Does it work if my income is tight?

Yes, and that is rather the point. The method changes the order of the payments you already make. Extra money speeds it up, but it is not a requirement.

I have student loans. Does that change things?

The guide covers them, including when to leave a federal loan on its existing plan rather than pulling it into the snowball. That decision alone is worth more than the price.

Is it just the avalanche method?

It starts there and then adapts, because the mathematically optimal order is not always the one people can stick to. The calculator shows both routes and the difference in months and dollars, so you can choose with your eyes open.
