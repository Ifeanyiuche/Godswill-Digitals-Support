The math nobody walks you through

Why the total refuses to move

Interest is charged on the balance you carry, so the first slice of every payment goes to the lender before a penny touches what you owe.

The Federal Reserve puts the average rate on cards that charge interest at about twenty two percent.

At that rate, a large share of every minimum payment is rent on money you already spent.

Now watch what the minimum does as you pay it.

Most issuers set it at around one percent of the balance plus that month's interest, so as the balance falls, the minimum falls too.

It feels like progress.

In practice, the payment shrinks almost as fast as the debt, and the last few thousand dollars take years.

Spread any extra money across four balances, and a second problem appears.

You pay a little extra into all of them, which feels responsible and productive, and it means no single balance clears quickly.

Nothing gets closed early, so no payment gets freed up to attack the next one.

You stay busy and you stay still.

The order then decides the last stretch.

The money going out of the account is identical in the lower two bars.

The only thing that changed between them is which balance receives the extra, and in what sequence.

Paying the highest rate first saves the most money, and research from Northwestern's Kellogg School of Management found that people who clear their smallest balances first are more likely to finish.
