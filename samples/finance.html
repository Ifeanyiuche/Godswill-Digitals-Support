The Payoff Order
For people carrying more than one balance

Your minimum payment shrinks every time your balance does. The shrinking is what keeps people in debt for decades.

Your statement even carries a legally required warning about it, in a small box most people have never read. Here is what that box is trying to tell you, and what to do about it.

You are not bad with money.

You pay what is due, every month, on time.

You have not missed anything.

You do the sensible thing and put a little extra toward whichever balance is bothering you most that week.

And the total has barely moved in two years.

None of that points to a discipline problem.

The cause is arithmetic, and it comes down to something most people were never taught.

Your minimum is recalculated every month from your balance, so each time the balance drops, the payment drops with it, and the finish line slides further away.

Hold the payment steady, point it in the right order, and twenty years come off the end.

Why the total refuses to move

Interest is charged on the balance you carry, so the first portion of every payment goes to the lender before a penny touches what you owe.

The Federal Reserve puts the average rate on cards that charge interest at about twenty two percent, and at that rate a large share of every minimum payment is rent on money you already spent.

Now watch what the minimum does as you pay it.

Most issuers set it at around one percent of the balance plus that month's interest, so as the balance falls, the minimum falls too.

It feels like progress.

In practice the payment shrinks almost as fast as the debt, and the last few thousand dollars take years.

The order then decides the last stretch.

Paying the highest rate first saves the most money, and research from Northwestern's Kellogg School of Management found that people who clear their smallest balances first are more likely to finish.
