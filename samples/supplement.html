The night shift

What your body is supposed to do while you sleep, and why it stops

Every night, your body runs a kind of night shift.

Most of the important work happens in one stage of sleep, the deepest one.

That is when growth hormone is released, when tissue gets repaired, and when the systems that decide whether you burn fuel or store it get reset for the next day.

Here is the part most people are never told.

That deep stage starts disappearing far earlier than you would guess.

When researchers at the University of Chicago studied 149 healthy men, deep sleep made up nearly 20 percent of the night before age 25 and less than 5 percent after 35.

Growth hormone, which is released mainly during deep sleep, fell by about 75 percent over the same years.

Deep sleep keeps declining from your thirties onward, and by your fifties many people get less than half of what they had at twenty five.

You can lie in bed for eight full hours and barely touch it.

And you would never know, because you were asleep the whole time.

Three things drive that decline, and each one makes the next one worse.

11pmSIGNAL

The "it is night" signal gets quieter

Melatonin production falls with age, so the message that tells your body night has arrived gets weaker every decade.

You take longer to drop off, and you spend more of the night in the lighter stages, where very little repair work happens.

3amALARM

The stress hormone refuses to switch off

Cortisol, the stress hormone, should fall to its lowest point in the evening.

The same Chicago research found that as sleep deteriorates with age, evening cortisol stays higher than it should, and night-time awakenings become more frequent and longer.

The 3am wake-up has a biological cause.

Your body stays on alert when it should be shutting down, which helps explain why you wake at three and cannot get back under.

7amBILL

The repair work never gets finished

Less deep sleep means less growth hormone, less tissue repair, and a metabolism that starts each day without ever finishing the last one.

You do not feel it on any single morning.

You see it in the mirror months later.

The figures above come from Eve Van Cauter's team at the University of Chicago, published in the Journal of the American Medical Association.

None of it is fringe science.

It is documented sleep physiology, published in one of the most respected medical journals in the world.

And it gets worse if you are trying to lose weight.

A separate University of Chicago study found that after just two nights of short sleep, the hormone that signals fullness fell by 18 percent and the hormone that drives hunger rose by 28 percent.

Picture trying to stick to a diet while your own appetite hormones pull in the other direction.

Very few people connect any of it to the reason the diet that worked at thirty does nothing at fifty.

So the next thing you try should work on your nights, not your plate.

L-theanine 200 MG

An amino acid found in tea leaves.

On its ownIt improves how rested you feel on waking.

In the blendIt supplies the temperature cue that the calming ingredients cannot provide.

Melatonin 0.5 MG

We kept this dose low on purpose.

In the blendA physiological dose nudges the signal instead of overriding it, which lets the other seven do their work.

Apigenin 50 MG

The compound in chamomile most associated with its calming reputation, supplied as a measured dose instead of whatever a tea bag happens to contain.

On its ownIt brings mild relaxation.

In the blendIt stretches the calming window through the first sleep cycle rather than just the first hour.

Vitamin B6 10 MG

Your body needs it to convert the amino acids above into the neurotransmitters that do the actual work.

On its ownIt is essential and rarely noticed.

In the blendB6 is a cofactor the body uses to make neurotransmitters such as serotonin and GABA, which is why it sits alongside the amino acids.
