Why almost everybody stalls in the same three places

After watching a lot of people attempt this, the failures are remarkably consistent. It is nearly always one of these, and rarely anything to do with talent.

1

Choosing forever

You have four ideas and no way to rank them, so you research all four and start none. The fix is a decision rule, not more thinking.

2

Building too much

The first version grows into a forty module monster because scope has no boundary. The fix is deciding what is out before you start, in writing.

3

Freezing at the price

It is built, and now naming a number feels like asking a favour. The fix is a pricing method that removes the guessing, so it stops feeling personal.

What six weeks looks like

One task a week. Each one has a deliverable, and you cannot move on without it. That constraint is the entire product.

Week 1

Pick the thing

Run your ideas through a scoring sheet that weighs demand against how fast you can build it. You finish the week with one idea and three parked.

Week 2

Draw the boundary

Write down what the product does and, more importantly, what it deliberately does not do. This is the week that prevents the forty module monster.

Week 3

Build the ugly version

Everything, start to finish, at rough quality. No design, no polish. The goal is a complete thing that works, not a good looking fragment.

Week 4

Put it in front of five people

Five real humans use it and tell you where they got stuck. You fix only what more than one person tripped on, and ignore the rest.

Week 5

Price it and write the page

The pricing method, then a sales page structure you fill in rather than invent. Templates included, because a blank page at week five is where people quit.

Week 6

Sell it

Checkout connected, delivery tested, and a launch sequence you send to whoever you have, even if that is forty people and your old colleagues.

You also get the finished examples. Every template comes filled in as well as blank, using a real product built the same way, so you can see what a completed version actually looks like before you attempt your own. Working from an example is considerably faster than working from instructions.

Before you ask

I do not have an audience.

Week six assumes you do not. The launch sequence is written for a small list of people who already know you, because that is where nearly every first sale actually comes from.

I do not know what to sell.

That is week one, and it is the week most people find the most useful. The scoring sheet usually surfaces something you had already dismissed as too obvious.

Is this passive income?

No. Building it takes six weeks of real work, and selling it takes ongoing effort. Anyone telling you otherwise is selling you the dream rather than the product.

What if I fall behind?

Nothing expires. The weeks are a pace, not a lock. People with full time jobs often run it across ten or twelve weeks and finish perfectly well.
