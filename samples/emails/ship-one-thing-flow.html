About 3% of people finish the online courses they start
MIT and Harvard counted. I was part of the other 97% for years.

Hi {first name},

In 2019, two researchers from MIT published an analysis in the journal Science covering years of online courses run by MIT and Harvard.

In the most recent year they studied, just over 3 percent of participants completed their course.

More than half of the people who registered never opened the course at all.

I am not judging anybody here, because I was a proud member of that majority for years.

My downloads folder held a copywriting course I had watched two modules of, a Notion template bundle I opened exactly once, and a very expensive masterclass on "finishing what you start" that, I am sorry to report, I did not finish.

None of that was wasted because the material was bad.

Most of it was good.

I kept buying the next course because I had not finished the last one, and buying felt close enough to progress to quiet the guilt for about a week.

Here is what those numbers tell you.

If more information made people finish things, completion rates would be enormous, because information has never been cheaper.

People stall for other reasons: too many options, no boundary on the scope, and no deadline anyone holds them to.

Over the next few days I will show you the three specific places first products stall, and how to get past each one.

Leah

P.S. Go and look at your downloads folder.

Count the courses you started and did not finish.

First products die in the same 3 places

Hi {first name},

When people try to build their first digital product, they almost always stall in one of three places.

  1. They get stuck choosing.

    Four ideas and no way to rank them means researching all four and starting none.

  2. They build too much.

    Version one grows into a forty-module monster because nobody drew a boundary around it.

  3. They freeze at the price.

    The product is finished, and suddenly naming a number feels like asking a stranger for a favor.

Each of those has a boring, practical fix: a decision rule, a written boundary and a pricing method that takes the guesswork out.

The scoring sheet you downloaded solves the first one.

The other two are where Ship One Thing comes in.

Why I refuse to sell you more information

Hi {first name},

Ship One Thing runs for six weeks, with one task per week, and you cannot move on until you have produced that week's deliverable.

In week one you pick the idea.

In week two you write down everything it will not do.

In week three you build an ugly but complete version.

In week four five real people use it and tell you where they got stuck.

In week five you price it and write the sales page, and in week six you sell it.

That structure carries the whole course.

It turns "I have been meaning to" into a listing with a price on it, which is the only kind of product that ever makes money.

"But I do not have an audience"
You need five people in week four and a short list in week six.

Hi {first name},

I hear this objection more than any other, so let us deal with it properly.

You do not need an audience to finish a product.

You need five people to try it in week four, and a list to send a launch email to in week six, even if that list is forty people and a few old colleagues from your last job.

Think about who is most likely to buy a first product.

A stranger who found you online has no reason to trust you yet.

A former colleague who watched you solve this exact problem for years already knows you are good at it.

Your first buyers are far more likely to come from the second group than the first.

The audience comes later, once you have something finished to point people towards.

Trying to build it first, with nothing to sell, is how people spend a year posting on social media and never launch anything.

Week six of Ship One Thing includes the launch sequence I would send to that small list, filled in with a real example so you can see how it reads before you write your own.
