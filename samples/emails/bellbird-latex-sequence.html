If you cool your home with an evaporative, water-cooled air conditioner, allergy specialists have a warning worth hearing.

Those units release water into the air, which can push up indoor dust mite and mould levels.

Unflued gas heaters do the same thing in winter.

Humidity in the room is one half of the picture, and the other half is what your mattress does with the moisture your own body adds every night.

The cosiest thing on your bed might be the worst thing for your nose
Allergy experts say it should come off.

Hi {first name},

When a mattress starts to feel tired, most of us do the same thing.

We add a layer.

It might be a wool underlay, a sheepskin, a thick topper or an extra quilt folded at the foot of the bed.

It feels like an upgrade, and on a cold night it is lovely.

The expert advice here is blunt.

They recommend removing sheepskins and woollen underlays from the bed and the bedroom altogether, because those materials provide an ideal environment for dust mites.

That was hard for me to read, because I love a sheepskin.

The reason comes down to warmth, moisture and somewhere to hide.

Dust mites are far more common in coastal cities and towns, where there is more moisture in the air, than in drier inland areas.

Every soft, warm layer you add to the bed gives them one more comfortable place to settle, and every layer that cannot go through a hot wash makes the problem harder to control.

The mattress underneath all those layers is the biggest soft furnishing in the house, and most people never think about what it is made of or whether any of it can be washed.

We designed Bellbird so that you would not need the extra layers at all, and so the part closest to your face could go straight into a hot wash.

Allergy specialists suggest an unusual trick for soft toys that cannot go in a hot wash.

Pop the toy in a bag and leave it in the freezer overnight, which kills the dust mites living in it.

The catch is that freezing does not remove the allergen they have already left behind, so the toy still needs a wash afterwards.

The same problem applies to the bed your child sleeps on every night, and that one does not fit in the freezer.

Your next mattress started life as a tree
It gets tapped, whipped, baked and aired before it reaches you.

Hi {first name},

Most people have no idea what is inside their mattress, so let me tell you about ours, because the story is stranger than you might expect.

Natural latex starts as a milky sap from the rubber tree.

Workers tap the bark with a shallow cut, much like tapping a maple tree for syrup, and the sap runs into a small cup over a few hours.

The tree is not cut down, and it keeps producing sap for years.

That sap is then whipped into a froth, a bit like beating egg whites, poured into a mould and baked until it sets into foam.

The mould is lined with metal pins, and those pins leave the rows of small holes you can see running through every latex core.

There are two main ways to make the foam.

The Dunlop method is the older one, and it produces a denser, firmer foam that tends to sit at the bottom of a mattress for support.

The Talalay method adds a vacuum and a flash freeze before baking, which gives a lighter, springier foam that often goes on top for comfort.

After baking, the foam is washed several times.

We then let every core air out for a fortnight before it ships, which is why it arrives smelling far less like a tyre shop than people expect.

I find it quite satisfying that the most important thing in your bedroom began as sap running down the side of a tree.
