Your minimum payment shrinks every month, and that keeps you in debt
It feels like progress, and it's the reason the debt lasts for decades.

Hi {first name},

Most card issuers calculate your minimum payment the same way.

They take about one percent of your balance and add that month's interest.

The Federal Reserve puts the average rate on cards that charge interest at around 22 percent.

Run that for a while and you notice something strange.

As your balance goes down, your minimum payment goes down with it.

Every month the statement asks for a little less, which feels like good news.

In practice the payment shrinks almost as fast as the debt, and the last few thousand dollars take years.

Here's what that looks like on a real-world example.

Picture $24,000 spread across three credit cards charging 22 to 27.5 percent and a personal loan at 11.9 percent.

The first month's minimums add up to about $689.

  • Pay only the minimums, and the debt takes about 25 years to clear, with roughly $40,800 paid in interest.
  • Keep paying a fixed $750 every month, just $61 more than that first statement asked for, and the debt is gone in about four years, with around $12,100 in interest.

The only difference between those two outcomes is refusing to let the payment shrink.

Nobody has to earn more, refinance or give up coffee.

There's a second decision that controls the last stretch, which is the order the extra money goes in.

I'll cover that on Thursday, because the research on it contains a real surprise.

The math says one thing. Northwestern found people do better with another.
Highest rate first saves the most. Smallest balance first gets more people to the finish.

Hi {first name},

Once your payment stops shrinking, you have extra money to aim at one balance at a time.

Which one should go first?

Mathematically, the answer is the balance with the highest interest rate.

In the $24,000 example from yesterday, aiming the extra at the highest-rate card first saves about $1,100 more than spreading it evenly across every balance.

Real people don't always behave like spreadsheets, and two researchers at Northwestern University's Kellogg School of Management tested it.

David Gal and Blake McShane studied thousands of people in a debt settlement program and found that the ones who cleared their smallest balances first were more likely to eliminate their debt completely.

Each closed account gave them a win they could see, and those wins kept them going.

So the right order depends on you.

If you're disciplined and motivated by numbers, go highest rate first.

If you've started plans before and drifted off them, the small-wins route may be the one you finish.

The Payoff Order calculator shows you both routes side by side, with your own balances, your own debt-free date and the difference in dollars and months, so you can choose with your eyes open.

A 15-minute phone call that works for 84% of people who make it

Here's something you can do this week, even if you never buy anything from me.

Call the card with your highest rate and ask for a lower one.

In a 2025 LendingTree survey of 2,050 American consumers, 84 percent of cardholders who had asked for a lower interest rate in the past year got one, and the average reduction was 6.3 percentage points.

That was the highest success rate in the six years they've been running the survey.

On a $9,000 balance at 25 percent, a 6.3-point cut saves roughly $560 in interest over the next year, from one phone call.

So why doesn't everyone do it?

Most people never call because they don't know what to say, and the ones who do often accept the first "no" they hear.

The first person you reach usually can't approve the change, and knowing how to ask for someone who can makes a big difference.

The Payoff Order includes four word-for-word scripts for that call: the opening request, what to say when you're told no, how to mention a competing offer, and how to ask about a hardship program if money is tight.
