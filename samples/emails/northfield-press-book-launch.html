The lunch table went quiet
I had been a manager for four days.

Hi {first name},

Four days after I was promoted, I sat down at the lunch table I had sat at for three years, and two conversations stopped at the same moment.

Nobody was rude.

Someone asked how the new job was going.

Someone else remembered an urgent email and headed back to their desk.

I went home that night feeling as though I had lost something I did not know I was allowed to miss.

It took me far too long to understand what had happened.

Nothing was wrong with the team, and nothing was wrong with me.

The relationships had changed shape, and everyone at that table was working out the new rules at the same time, without anyone saying them out loud.

The stakes are higher than they feel in the moment.

Gallup has measured the engagement of millions of employees over two decades, and it estimates that managers account for at least 70 percent of the variance in how engaged their teams are.

The people at that lunch table would soon be having a better or worse working life depending on how I handled the next few months.

The new managers who handle this well tend to do one thing early.

They say, out loud and in private, that something has changed.

Tomorrow I will show you exactly how that conversation goes.

Dana

Have this conversation with a friend you now manage
Have it this week, before anything goes wrong.

Hi {first name},

If one of your new direct reports is also a friend, have this conversation this week, while everything is still fine.

It becomes much harder after the first problem.

Keep it short, find somewhere private, and say three things.

  1. Tell them what is not changing.

    "I still value our friendship, and I am not going to turn into a different person."

  2. Tell them what is changing.

    "There will be times I have to make calls you will not agree with, and I will have to make them anyway."

  3. Tell them what you need from them.

    "If something I do feels off, tell me directly. I would much rather hear it from you than second-hand."

Then stop talking and let them respond.

In my experience, most people are relieved that you raised it first, because they have been wondering how to raise it themselves.

A word of warning about the most tempting line in this conversation, which is "nothing will change between us".

Your friend will remember it the first time you have to give them difficult feedback, and it will make that conversation far harder than it needed to be.

Chapter 3 of Promoted covers what to do when this conversation goes badly, and it lists the other well-meaning phrases that tend to backfire.

Dana
