A letter to the teacher who is tired

You did not lose your passion for teaching. You lost your Sundays.

If you have your CELTA, a room full of students who like you, and a quiet dread that arrives every Sunday evening, this letter is for you. It is longer than most. I wrote it that way on purpose.

Let me ask you four questions. If the answer to any of them is yes, you and I are going to get along.

  • Do you feel underprepared in front of a class, even though you have the certificate that says you are not?
  • Are you still hunting for the balance between planning a lesson properly and actually having a life?
  • Has the enthusiasm you started with quietly turned into getting through the week?
  • Do you suspect you have been teaching roughly the same way for three years because there has been no time to learn a better one?

If you said yes to those, I want to tell you something that took me an embarrassingly long time to work out myself.

You are not bad at this. You are under-resourced.

There is a difference, and the difference matters enormously, because one of those has a solution and the other one just makes you feel awful on a Sunday evening.

Why good teachers burn out, and it is almost never about the students

Think about what your certification actually gave you. A month, maybe two. Enough grammar to stay ahead of an intermediate class. A handful of activities. Six observed lessons and a pass.

What it could not give you, because no four week course could, is the thing experienced teachers have that you do not: a deep library of activities they can reach for without thinking.

That library is the whole difference. A teacher with one takes twenty minutes to plan a lesson because they already know four things that will work with this group and this language point. A teacher without one takes two and a half hours, because every lesson is built from scratch, from a blank page, on a Sunday.

Nobody quits teaching because they stopped caring about students. They quit because of what the job costs them outside the classroom.

And here is the part that turns tiredness into something worse.

When planning takes that long, you start to economise. You reach for the coursebook because it is there. You repeat the activity that went alright last term. You skip the bit you know they would enjoy because it needs materials you do not have time to make.

None of those choices is wrong on its own. Taken together, over a year, they slowly turn a teacher who was interesting into a teacher who is adequate. And you feel it happening. That is the worst part. You can feel yourself becoming less good at the thing you wanted to be good at, and you are too tired to do anything about it.

Then the guilt arrives, and the guilt is what actually ends careers.

What fifteen years of watching this problem taught me

My name is Richard. I have been training teachers since my DELTA, which is now over fifteen years ago, and in that time I have sat at the back of a great many classrooms.

What I noticed is that the teachers who lasted were not the most gifted ones. Some of the most naturally talented people I trained left the profession inside four years.

The ones who lasted had systems. They had a way of planning that did not start from nothing. They had a set of activities that worked across levels, so a new group did not mean a new curriculum. They had a method for making a coursebook unit interesting without rewriting it. They were not working less hard. They were working from a foundation instead of from scratch.

So I spent a long time writing down what those teachers did, and turning it into something a teacher in their second year could pick up and use in the same week.

That is what this programme is. Forty hours, practical from the first minute, and built entirely around the things I have watched keep good teachers in the classroom.

What is actually inside

No theory for the sake of theory. Every section ends with something you can use in your next lesson.

01

Planning that takes twenty minutes

A repeatable structure that works for any level and any language point, so you stop designing each lesson from a blank page. Includes the planning templates I use, and worked examples at A2, B1 and B2 so you can see the shape rather than just read about it.

02

The activity library

Over ninety classroom activities, sorted by what they achieve rather than by topic. Each one has a level range, a time, the materials needed, and the moment in a lesson where it works best. This is the thing experienced teachers carry in their heads, written down.

03

Making a coursebook unit worth teaching

Most of us are handed a book and told to use it. This covers how to take a dull unit and make it live, what to cut without leaving a gap, and how to build in real world content so a lesson is about something rather than about English for no apparent reason.

04

Real classroom observation

Filmed lessons with my commentary running alongside, including the moments that did not go to plan and what I did about them. Teachers tell me this is the part that changed the most, because watching a lesson go slightly wrong and recover is worth more than watching a perfect one.

05

Correction and feedback that students act on

When to correct, when to leave it, and how to give feedback that changes what a student does next week rather than just marking what they did wrong last week.

06

Standing out in a crowded market

What schools actually look for when they choose between two similarly qualified teachers, how to talk about your teaching in an interview, and how to build a reputation that means the good jobs find you.

Questions teachers ask me

I only have my CELTA. Is this too advanced?

No, and newly certified teachers often get the most from it, because they have not yet built habits that need undoing. Everything assumes you can teach and does not assume you have been doing it for a decade.

I have been teaching for twelve years. Will I learn anything?

Honestly, that depends on you. Experienced teachers tell me the observation module and the activity library are where they find things, and that the planning section mostly confirms what they already do. If you are already efficient and still enjoying it, you may not need this. I would rather say that than take your money.

How long does it take?

Forty hours of material, and most people spread it across six to ten weeks alongside teaching. You can also take one module, use it, and come back. Nothing expires and there is no cohort to keep up with.

Is it recognised? Will it get me a job?

It is professional development, not a qualification, so it will not replace a DELTA on paper. What it changes is how you teach and how you talk about your teaching, which is what actually decides most interviews between two similarly qualified candidates.

I teach online, not in a classroom.

Most of it transfers directly and the activity library flags which activities need adapting for a screen. The observation footage is in-person, so you will be translating some of that yourself.

One last thing

You did not get into this to become efficient at lesson planning. You got into it because at some point a room full of people understood something because of you, and it felt like the best possible way to spend a Tuesday.

That feeling is still available. It is just currently buried under fourteen hours a week of admin that nobody trained you to handle.
